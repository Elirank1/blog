**60-second read.** Between May and October 2026, Anthropic and OpenAI each built — or backed — a company whose entire job is putting AI to work inside other companies. Private equity funded both. By late October, Anthropic was reportedly discussing a second one, this time for Europe. None of this is about better models. It's an admission that a great model and a working deployment are two different products, and that the gap between them is large enough to organize billions of dollars of capital around. What's genuinely new here isn't the *work* — forward-deployed engineering and AI implementation consulting already existed. What's new is the *structure*: frontier labs, private equity and deployment specialists combining directly, at this scale, inside purpose-built companies. The open question is whether that structure stays expert-shaped the way it's built today, or whether the hardest part of the work — the judgment, not the code — eventually gets absorbed into software. That's my thesis, not a fact, and I try to be as hard on it as I can before I ask you to take it seriously.

## 1. The 2026 timeline

Here's what's confirmed, in order, with the qualifications that matter attached directly to each claim rather than buried in a footnote.

**[March 12](https://www.anthropic.com/news/claude-partner-network)** — Anthropic launches the **Claude Partner Network**: free membership for consultancies and system integrators that bring Claude work to enterprises, backed by an initial $100M for 2026 in training, support and co-marketing — infrastructure for an outside ecosystem, not Anthropic deploying directly.

**[May 4](https://www.anthropic.com/news/enterprise-ai-services-company)** — Anthropic, Blackstone and Hellman & Friedman, with a consortium behind them (Goldman Sachs, General Atlantic, Leonard Green, Apollo, GIC, Sequoia), announce a new AI services company for mid-sized companies that "lack the in-house resources to build and run deployments." Anthropic's CFO, Krishna Rao: *"Enterprise demand for Claude is significantly outpacing any single delivery model."* Unnamed at this point; no funding figure disclosed.

**[May 11](https://openai.com/index/openai-launches-the-deployment-company/), seven days later** — OpenAI launches **the Deployment Company**: majority-owned by OpenAI, more than $4 billion in initial investment, 19 founding partners led by TPG. The same announcement states OpenAI has **agreed to acquire** Tomoro, an applied-AI consultancy, subject to customary closing conditions — bringing roughly 150 working Forward Deployed Engineers and clients including Tesco, Virgin Atlantic and Supercell into the new company. The same page states one figure this essay returns to in section 5: OpenAI's investment and consulting partners already **sponsor more than 2,000 businesses**.

**[May 14](https://www.tpg.com/news-and-insights/bloomberg-deals-david-trujillo-on-tpgs-partnership-with-openai-to-launch-deployco)** — TPG partner David Trujillo names the gap this industry exists to close: *"these models have progressed far beyond enterprise's ability to know how to use them just yet."* A capital partner, not a lab, saying the constraint isn't the model.

**[June 3](https://www.anthropic.com/news/services-track-partner-hub)** — Anthropic adds a **Services Track** to the Partner Network — Select → Preferred → Global Premier, gated on deployed production customers, not training completions. 40,000+ firms had applied, 10,000+ consultants certified. *"A successful pilot is not the same as a system a business can run on."*

**[July 15](https://www.ode.com/press/anthropic-blackstone-and-hellman-friedman-introduce-ode-with-anthropic-an-enterprise-ai-services-firm)** — The May 4 company launches under its name: **Ode**, built on **Fractional AI**, an applied-AI firm Anthropic and its partners acquired that same May. Founders Chris Taylor and Eddie Siegel stay on as CEO/CTO. Taylor: *"the challenge is making it real."* No funding figure or team size disclosed.

**[October 2](https://www.anthropic.com/news/claude-frontier-academy)** — Anthropic commits **$100M to Claude Frontier Academy**: a target of **10,000 Frontier Deployed Engineers by the end of 2027**, already training in San Francisco, New York and London.

**October 23** — The *Financial Times* reports — relayed here through corroborating secondary coverage, since the article itself is paywalled — that Anthropic and the European buyout firm **Hg** are discussing a second deployment-style venture, this time based in London and Berlin. Neither company had confirmed it at the time the story ran. If it happens, it would be **Anthropic's second** venture of this kind (after Ode), and the **third** such venture industry-wide once OpenAI's Deployment Company is counted alongside it.

**October 28** — Analyst Guillermo Flor, writing independently on Substack, argues that the labs are becoming direct competitors to Big Four–style consultancies. He also cites a specific dollar figure for planned investment behind Ode. I'm deliberately **not repeating that figure here**: it isn't disclosed anywhere by Ode or Anthropic, and a single analyst's unconfirmed estimate doesn't belong in the factual spine of this piece. It's in the ledger, clearly labeled as his, for anyone who wants to chase it down themselves.

That's seven confirmed events in eight months, two of them forming new companies with billions of disclosed committed capital, from two labs that are supposedly competing primarily on model quality. They're also, very visibly, racing to stand up the same kind of company around that model.

None of this means "AI deployment" is a six-month-old industry. Forward-deployed engineering, AI implementation consulting and systems-integration work predate every event on this list by years — Accenture, Deloitte and a long list of boutique AI consultancies were doing this work before any lab built a company around it. **What's actually new in 2026 is the structure**: frontier labs and private equity combining directly, inside purpose-built companies, at this scale, with the lab's own engineers and the lab's own brand attached. That's a narrower, more defensible claim than "a new industry was born in May," and it's the one this essay is actually making.

## 2. Why a great model isn't a working deployment

It's worth being concrete about the gap, because "deployment is hard" is the kind of sentence that sounds true and explains nothing.

Inside a real organization, the hard part was never only the integration code. Wiring a frontier model into a company's actual systems is frequently a real bottleneck in its own right, not a solved problem beneath mention. Legacy infrastructure, inconsistent data formats, access controls that were never designed with an external API caller in mind, and latency or cost budgets that don't survive contact with production traffic are genuine engineering work, and models getting better doesn't make any of it easier.

What model improvements help with less, even as they improve, is the layer above the engineering: data that's technically available but practically unusable without someone who knows which fields lie; processes that exist in three versions — the one in the handbook, the one people actually follow, and the one that only runs during the last week of the quarter; decision rights that were never written down because the two people who hold them have just always sorted it out between themselves; an evaluation bar nobody has agreed on, so "it works" means something different to the VP who approved the budget and the analyst who has to live with the output; and the plain organizational fact that someone's job description changes if this works, which means someone has a quiet incentive for it not to.

I'll also say the more interesting, more honest version of the claim: a better model genuinely *can* help with parts of this — synthesizing a messy pile of interview transcripts into a first-draft process map, flagging inconsistencies across versions of a policy document, drafting the options for a decision rather than making it. None of that shows up in a capability benchmark, and all of it is real progress. What a better model does *not* do on its own is resolve who has the authority to approve the change, what counts as sufficient evidence that it worked, or who absorbs the consequences if it didn't. That's the part that doesn't move with model quality, and it's the part this whole industry is actually organized around.

## 3. The industry's shape: three parts, one each

What's forming is a specific three-part structure, and the parts are complementary in a way that's worth being precise about, because each one is solving a different constraint.

**Frontier labs** bring the technology and, less obviously, the credibility — a Claude or OpenAI badge on a deployment firm is itself a sales asset, because it answers "why trust this model with our core process" before the question is asked.

**Private equity** brings more than a check. It brings a *potential access channel*: a portfolio of operating companies that a deployment firm can approach with an existing relationship rather than a cold sales cycle. That claim needs a careful ceiling, though — portfolio access is not the same as signed contracts, and a PE firm's operating companies are not captive customers simply because the fund has a stake in both. What private equity plausibly does bring is executive-level relationships at the CEO/CFO level that a standalone AI startup wouldn't have on day one, and an incentive structure built around repeatable operating improvement across many companies — which is a genuinely useful muscle for deployment-at-scale, even if it converts to revenue at an unknown rate.

**Deployment operators** — the FDEs, the Fractional AIs, the Tomoros — bring the one thing neither of the other two has: people who have actually sat inside an organization and made a specific AI system work against its specific mess. That's not a commodity skill yet. It's also, notably, the part being *acquired* rather than built from scratch in both deals above — Ode is built on the acquired Fractional AI team, and OpenAI's Deployment Company has agreed to acquire Tomoro's team and client base rather than hiring and training entirely from zero.

I want to be explicit about where documentation ends and my own reading begins: what's documented is the structure above. *Why* it's a good structure — that each party is supplying the one asset it has a real advantage in, instead of trying to build all three — is my inference about the logic behind these deals, not something any of these companies has stated as their stated reasoning. I think it's a reasonable inference. It's still mine, not theirs.

## 4. What an FDE's job actually is

"Forward Deployed Engineer" gets used loosely enough that it's worth unpacking into what the job is actually built from.

**Discovery** is sitting with the people who do the work — not the people who manage the people who do the work — until you can describe the process more accurately than the organization's own documentation does. **Judgment** is deciding which of the twenty things you found is worth solving first, which require a human sign-off forever and which don't, and where the organization's stated priorities and its actual incentives disagree. **Feasibility** is the engineering: can this actually be built against these systems, this data, this latency and cost budget — and, as I noted above, this step alone can be a genuine bottleneck, not a formality. **Proof** is building a version narrow enough to be trusted and running it next to the existing process until the evidence, not the pitch, says it's ready. **Operation** is what happens after launch — monitoring, handling edge cases nobody anticipated, deciding when something that looked fine in week one needs to be pulled back. **Learning** is whether anything from this engagement makes the next one faster, which is currently mostly a function of whether the *same person* happens to work on both — and it's the question the rest of this essay is actually about.

I won't claim discovery and judgment are categorically harder than feasibility and operation across every engagement — that varies by industry and by how messy the underlying systems are. What I will claim, more narrowly, is that discovery and judgment are the stages where none of the three countertrend products in section 6 claims to replace a human yet, which is a specific, checkable fact rather than a sweeping one.

## 5. The economics, stated honestly

None of Ode, OpenAI's Deployment Company or Anthropic has published engagement-level economics — revenue per deployment, margin, cost structure. I'm not going to invent numbers to fill that gap. What I can do is lay out the actual structure of the problem, because it's more useful than it sounds, and it's where the one genuinely strong data point from this research belongs.

[OpenAI's own announcement](https://openai.com/index/openai-launches-the-deployment-company/) states that its investment and consulting partners for the Deployment Company already sponsor more than 2,000 businesses, with consulting and integrator partners reaching many thousands more. That's a real number from a primary source, and it's analytically interesting for a specific reason: if a deployment mechanism improves even slightly, and that improvement can be distributed across a network that size without rebuilding it from scratch for each company, the economics of this industry look completely different than if every engagement starts from zero. But I want to immediately narrow what that number actually proves, because it's easy to overstate: **sponsorship reach is not signed demand, signed demand is not revenue, and none of those 2,000+ businesses has been confirmed as an active paying Deployment Company engagement.** It's a plausible distribution channel. It is not evidence of realized economics.

With that caveat in place, here's a conceptual unit-economics model for one engagement — not a forecast, a framework for thinking about where the cost actually sits:

- **Expert intervention hours** — discovery, judgment and proof, from section 4; the hardest to reduce.
- **Implementation effort** — the feasibility engineering; partially reusable across engagements if the underlying systems are similar (e.g., two companies on the same ERP), largely not if they aren't.
- **QA and validation** — the shadow-mode comparison against the existing process, which has to happen regardless of how reusable the build was.
- **Rework** — the cost of the first version being wrong, which is a function of how good discovery and judgment were, not of model quality.
- **Model and tool cost** — the one line item that reliably falls as models get cheaper and faster, and also, per section 1's OpenAI figure, probably the smallest line item relative to the others.
- **Customer-specific integration and maintenance** — ongoing, and the main reason "finished" deployments still carry cost after launch.

The honest, unresolved question — the one I don't think anyone, including the companies in this essay, has answered publicly — is which of these six lines compounds in software's favor as the number of engagements grows, and which stays stubbornly linear with headcount. I'd guess implementation effort and QA compound the most readily, because they're closer to engineering. I'd guess expert intervention hours compound the least readily, because that's where discovery and judgment live. That's a guess, not a finding, and section 8 is where I try to make it more rigorous than a guess.

## 6. The countertrend: deployment is already being productized, by other people

It would be wrong — and it would make for a weaker essay — to write this as if nobody has noticed that deployment work can be partly automated. Several companies are already doing it, with real, shipping product.

**[Wonderful](https://www.wonderful.ai/blog-articles/wonderful-raises-550m-series-c)** raised a $550M Series C in September at a $5B valuation to build what it calls an AI OS — a shared layer for agents, workflows and governed execution. Its own stated model is that forward-deployed engineers bring the *first* use case to production and then **transfer the work to the customer's own team**, rather than staying embedded indefinitely. CTO Roey Lalazar's framing — *"enterprises shouldn't have to replace everything they already have to become AI-native"* — is a direct bet against the embedded-expert-forever model.

**[Sierra's Ghostwriter](https://sierra.ai/product/ghostwriter)** goes further into automating the *build* itself: it turns plain-language instructions and existing documents into working agent configurations, generates its own test simulations, diagnoses failures and proposes fixes. Stated precisely: Ghostwriter automates configuration-authoring and test generation, and **explicitly keeps a human reviewing before anything ships** — that's stated on the product page itself, not an inference. That's real automation of parts of the feasibility and proof stages from section 4. It is not a claim, by Sierra or by this essay, that discovery or judgment is automated.

**[ServiceNow's AI Control Tower](https://investor.servicenow.com/news/news-details/2026/ServiceNow-expands-AI-Control-Tower-to-discover-observe-govern-secure-and-measure-AI-deployed-across-any-system-in-the-enterprise/default.aspx)**, which expanded significantly in May, is a more honest fit here as *adjacent* than as a direct competitor — it's a governance and observability layer for agents that already exist, not a tool for building or deploying them. It matters to this essay for a different reason: it's evidence that even the governance half of this problem is being built as software, fast, which raises my confidence that the deployment half eventually will be too — though I'll note that's my inference from the pattern, not a claim ServiceNow is making about itself.

None of this means the FDE companies are already obsolete. It does mean the premise of this essay — that deployment stays expert-shaped — has working counter-evidence today, in shipping product, not just in theory. Section 9 engages that counter-evidence directly rather than past it.

## 7. My two cents: from scaling people to compounding deployment intelligence

Here's my actual thesis, stated as a thesis, not a fact: **the deployment companies forming right now are solving 2026's problem with 2026's tool — expert hours — and the more interesting question is whether the judgment those experts apply can itself become reusable, software-defined intelligence, not just the code they write.**

The lazy version of this idea stops at "AI will automate consulting," which isn't a thesis, it's a vibe. The real version has to say precisely what moves from one engagement to the next, what blocks each step from moving further, and why simply having better agents doesn't automatically clear each block. That's what sections 8 and 9 try to do.

## 8. The four levels, and what actually blocks each transition

**Level 1 — Code and template reuse.** Integration code, UI components, common connector patterns. This is already happening everywhere, every consultancy has a component library, and it's the least interesting level because it was never the hard part (see section 2). It doesn't require anything specific to AI deployment; it's ordinary software engineering reuse.

**Level 2 — Delivery-process reuse.** Not the code, but the *method*: how you run a discovery interview so you actually surface the undocumented process instead of the official one; which test cases reliably catch the edge cases a given industry's workflows tend to have; how you structure a shadow-mode comparison so the evidence is actually convincing to a skeptical stakeholder. This is more valuable than level 1 and further along than people assume — Ghostwriter's auto-generated test simulations are arguably a level-2 product, not a level-1 one, because "how do you test an agent well" is methodology, not a template.

The barrier moving from level 1 to level 2: method only transfers if the *underlying problem shape* is similar enough across clients. A test-generation approach tuned for customer-service agents may simply not apply to a manufacturing-scheduling agent. More capable agents don't fix this on their own, because the mismatch isn't a capability gap, it's a problem-shape gap — the agent would need to recognize that this client's problem doesn't fit the method it knows, which is itself a judgment call.

**Level 3 — Decision-mechanism reuse.** Not the method for running a discovery interview, but the actual logic an experienced FDE uses to decide *what's worth automating first*, *what needs permanent human sign-off*, and *when there's enough evidence to expand scope*. This is qualitatively different from level 2: a method is a procedure you follow; a decision mechanism is the thing that decides which procedure applies, and when to deviate from it. I haven't seen anyone demonstrate this convincingly in public. I don't think it's close to solved.

The barrier here is sharper than at the previous transition: a decision mechanism trained or tuned on one organization's priorities, risk tolerance and incentive structure doesn't obviously generalize to an organization with different ones — and unlike a code template, you can't easily inspect a decision mechanism to tell whether it's overfit to the client it came from. More agent automation makes this *harder* to detect, not easier, because a more fluent, more confident-sounding agent is also better at producing a plausible-looking recommendation that happens to be wrong for this specific client. Confidence and correctness are not the same axis, and better models improve the first one more reliably than the second.

**Level 4 — Organizational recompilation.** The question underneath all three previous levels: is the workflow being automated even the right target? An FDE operating at level 4 isn't asking "how do we make this approval chain faster," they're asking "given the outcome this team is actually accountable for, and the constraints that are genuinely non-negotiable, what's the version of this workflow you'd design if you started today." That's a harder, more uncomfortable question to put in front of a client than "let's automate step 3," and I don't think it's the default mode of this work yet, anywhere, including in what I'm building.

The barrier at level 4 needs a sharper cut than "it's organizational, full stop." Two different things sit on top of each other here, and they don't compound the same way. *Implementing* a redesigned workflow requires authority that an external deployment team usually doesn't have and shouldn't be assumed to have — approval chains, budget, the standing to tell a VP their process is wrong. That part genuinely is a human-authority question, and no amount of better software grants an outside party the standing to act on its own recommendation. But *designing, evaluating and learning from* candidate organizational alternatives — generating the redesign options, scoring them against the stated constraints, comparing outcomes across past engagements — is a different kind of work, and nothing about it is inherently non-computational. A system could plausibly get better at proposing and evaluating level-4 redesigns long before anyone grants it the authority to implement one. The two should be tracked separately: compounding the analysis is one question; compounding the authority to act on it is another, and the second one is where the real ceiling sits.

**What actually compounds, stated plainly:** level 1 compounds freely and isn't interesting. Level 2 compounds within similar problem shapes and degrades across dissimilar ones. Level 3 compounds only if a mechanism can be shown to work across organizations with genuinely different incentives — which I haven't seen demonstrated — and the generalization claim itself is hard to verify because the mechanism's reasoning is opaque. Level 4's *analysis* layer could in principle compound like the others; its *authority to implement* cannot, because that's not a software property at all.

And underneath level 3 specifically sits a constraint worth being precise about rather than stating as a blanket legal fact, because three different things get collapsed into "data privacy" and they don't all block the same thing. **Data rights** govern company A's actual records — those don't travel to company B, full stop, and no deployment firm should be trying to move them. **Model-training rights** govern whether those records can be used to tune a shared model at all, which is a narrower, contract-specific question, not a universal prohibition. And **reuse of a generalized mechanism** — the abstracted shape of a judgment, stripped of company A's specific facts — is a third, separate question again: whether that abstraction still carries enough of company A's fingerprint to need its own authorization is a case-by-case determination, not something ruled out by data-protection law on its face. What's actually unresolved is whether anyone has built the abstraction step cleanly enough that the third category is genuinely clean of the first two — I haven't seen it demonstrated — and that, not a categorical legal bar, is the real ceiling on this part of the thesis.

## 9. Taking the counterargument seriously

I don't think it's honest to lay out four levels of potential reuse and then assume a standalone "deployment intelligence" company is the thing that captures them. There's a real, current counterargument, and it deserves equal weight, not a token paragraph before the conclusion.

The counterargument: platform companies and the labs themselves are the more likely place this intelligence ends up, not a separate category of company built around it. Wonderful's AI OS is explicitly trying to *absorb* levels 1 and 2 into a product the customer's own team eventually owns, rather than keep a deployment firm permanently in the loop. Sierra is doing the same thing for one vertical. And it would be strange, frankly, for Anthropic to spend $100M training 10,000 FDEs and *not* eventually try to encode some of what those FDEs learn back into Claude itself, or into tooling Claude ships — the lab has every incentive to internalize level 2 and level 3 into its own product rather than leave that value sitting with Ode, Fractional AI or any other outside operator.

If that's what happens, the "software-defined deployment layer" I'm gesturing at in section 8 isn't an independent company category at all — it's a feature of the next version of the model, or of the platforms built directly on top of it. I think that's a live possibility, not a strawman I'm knocking down to make my own thesis look better by comparison. The honest version of my own position is narrower than "a new category of company will emerge": it's that **someone** — a lab, a platform, an independent layer, or some combination — will eventually have to solve the level-3 generalization problem and the data-isolation constraint underneath it, and whoever does will matter more than whoever has the most FDEs on staff in 2026.

## 10. The harder question underneath this one

There's an assumption buried in almost everything written about this industry so far, including most of this essay: that the thing being deployed is a faster version of the organization's *existing* workflow. Level 4 above is where that assumption breaks, and I'd rather flag it as a direction than overclaim it as a solved idea — I haven't seen anyone, including us, prove this out at scale. But I think it's the more valuable question, and the one most of this new industry isn't yet positioned to ask its clients, because asking it requires a kind of authority — and a kind of trust — that takes longer to earn than a signed engagement letter.

## 11. What would actually prove — or break — this thesis

I'd rather be falsifiable than persuasive. Evidence that would make me more confident: expert intervention time per deployment going down across *successive* engagements with the same organization or similar organizations, with decision quality held stable or improving, not just speed; a documented case of a level-3 decision mechanism transferring across two genuinely different organizations without rebuilding from scratch and without their private data moving between them; and reproducible evaluation — the same proof standard applied consistently, not a different bar for each client story.

Evidence that would break it: **genericity collapse**, where anything reusable enough to transfer across clients turns out too generic to be useful for any one of them — the classic failure mode of every "reusable enterprise framework" ever attempted. **Hidden human intervention**, where a "software-compounding" story turns out to be humans quietly doing the judgment work off to the side while the product demo shows only the automated part. And, specifically for level 4, **authority refusal** — if, three years from now, no deployment firm or platform has meaningfully expanded its mandate from "automate the existing process" to "question whether it's the right process," that's evidence the organizational barrier there is harder than the technical one, which would mean this essay's most original claim was the least important one in practice.

## 12. What happens next

A few honest scenarios, not a prediction dressed up as one. The services-at-scale version wins outright, and in five years "AI deployment" is simply a mature professional-services category the way system integration is today — plausible, and arguably the safer bet given how much capital just backed exactly that. The platform-native version wins, per section 9, and companies like Wonderful's AI OS or the labs' own tooling quietly absorb most of levels 1 and 2, leaving a smaller, much higher-judgment human layer on top for levels 3 and 4. A standalone software-infrastructure layer emerges — something closer to what I'm personally exploring — that doesn't try to be the deployment company, but tries to solve the level-3 generalization problem directly. Or, most likely in my own reading, some combination: services for the hardest, most level-3-and-4 work, software absorbing the rest, and the boundary between those two moving every year as the generalization problem gets chipped away, not solved outright.

I don't think the frontier labs, the PE firms backing them, or the software companies racing to productize parts of this have settled that question yet either. Nobody's wrong so far. What's actually new is six to eight months old. The underlying profession isn't.

The question is no longer whether AI deployment will become a major industry. The capital, the companies and the talent are already moving — that part of this essay is reporting, not speculation.

The harder question is what that industry will learn to turn into software, and how much of the expertise required to transform an organization will still need to begin from scratch with every engagement. Nothing in the record above answers that yet. But it's the question worth tracking, because whoever answers it will matter more than whoever has the most Forward Deployed Engineers on staff this year.
