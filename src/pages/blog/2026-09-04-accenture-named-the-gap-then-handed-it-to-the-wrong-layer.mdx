Accenture and Wharton published a joint report this year called *The Age of Co-Intelligence*. Buried in it is the sentence the industry has been dancing around for eighteen months:

*"Intelligence may be scalable, but accountability is not."*

Read that again.

That is not a phrase from a Deeplica blog post. It is a phrase from a Tier-1 consultancy and a top-five business school, jointly, in a report aimed at Fortune 500 boards. The gap has been named. The naming was institutional. The framing was clean.

Then the report handed the fix to the wrong layer.

## What the report actually prescribed

The Accenture/Wharton prescription is that as agents scale, human leaders become *more* consequential. Leadership does not diminish — it deepens. Humans must decide what matters, set strategy, own the outcomes the agents execute. In a badly designed enterprise, one human ends up responsible for "an exponential cascade of outcomes they never saw coming." In a well-designed one, they stay ahead of the cascade by owning it harder.

Own it harder.

This is the same category error the productivity industry made with cognitive overload. The symptom was named correctly — workers reporting brain fry, productivity dropping below baseline past four tools — and the prescription was pace yourself, take breaks, meditate. As if the failure were a behavioral one. As if the fix belonged in the person.

The Accenture prescription belongs in the same folder. The symptom is named — accountability does not scale. The fix assigned to it is that leaders must become more accountable. Lead harder. Own more. Stay ahead of the cascade through the force of executive presence.

You cannot own an outcome you cannot trace.

## What accountability actually requires

An agent takes an action. A payment is sent. A customer is contacted. A record is modified. A resource is booked. To hold anyone accountable for that action later, four things have to be true:

You have to know what the agent committed to.
You have to know on whose authority it committed.
You have to know whether the commitment was fulfilled.
You have to know what happened downstream when it was — or wasn't.

None of those are leadership traits. They are properties of a substrate. Either the infrastructure captured the commitment at the moment it was made, or it didn't. If it didn't, no amount of "owning the outcome" retrieves the trace after the fact. The commitment was never a record. The record was only the action.

This is the distinction the report walks up to and does not name. Actions are logged. Commitments are not. The audit layer captured every call the agent made. It did not capture what the agent said it was going to do, on what authority, and against what open loop.

The accountability gap is not a leadership gap. It is a missing layer.

## What the numbers underneath actually say

The Cloud Security Alliance ran the survey Accenture cited around. Different vantage, same phenomenon, but the numbers are the part that should end the "own it harder" prescription on its own terms:

Sixty-five percent of enterprises had at least one agent-caused security incident in the past year.
Sixty-three percent cannot enforce purpose limits on the agents they've deployed — an agent scoped to draft customer service replies can read customer financial records in the same system, and nothing structurally stops it.
Sixty percent cannot terminate a misbehaving agent once it is running.
Eighty-two percent have agents in their environment they don't know about.

Multi-agent orchestration doubled inside a single quarter, from nine percent to eighteen percent of large US organizations.

Ask the Accenture prescription to work against those numbers. A leader is supposed to own the outcomes of agents they cannot enumerate, running in systems that cannot scope them, that they cannot stop, coordinating with other agents whose commitments are not recorded anywhere. The prescription is not a solution. It is a liability assignment.

That is what "accountability does not scale" actually means. It does not mean the humans in charge need to try harder. It means the substrate that would let them try is not there.

## Why the industry keeps reaching for the wrong layer

Because the right layer does not exist yet in most stacks, and naming what is missing is harder than naming who is responsible.

Every generation of enterprise infrastructure has had this same handoff. Databases before ACID transactions: developers were told to "be careful with concurrent writes." Networks before TCP: engineers were told to "handle retries in application code." Identity before SSO: users were told to "use strong passwords and don't reuse them." In each case the industry spent a decade prescribing discipline to the layer above before finally admitting the discipline belonged in a layer below.

Agents are in the same phase. Something is missing at the substrate level. The consultancies name the symptom and assign it upward — to leaders, to governance committees, to Chief AI Officers. Forrester is now predicting sixty percent of Fortune 100 companies will appoint a "head of AI governance" in 2026. That role will fail for the same reason a Chief Password Officer would have failed in 2005. The problem is not that nobody owns it. The problem is that there is nothing underneath to own.

## What the coordination layer actually is

Not a governance framework. Not a risk register. Not a role.

A coordination layer is the substrate that captures, for every agent action: what commitment it was made against, on whose authority, when it was opened, whether it has been closed, and what depends on it. It sits below the audit log and above the tool call. The audit log records that the agent invoked `send_payment`. The coordination layer records that the agent committed to executing a payment authorized by policy X against open loop Y, and it holds the loop open until closure is confirmed.

With that substrate, "who is accountable" is a query. Without it, it is a leadership exercise — which is to say, it is theater.

The Accenture report is right that accountability does not scale. It is right that the pattern is structural. It is wrong that the solution is human. Humans cannot scale accountability the same way they could not scale password memorization or manual transaction ordering. That is not a failing of humans. It is a description of what infrastructure is for.

## The wedge

There will be a wave of consulting engagements in 2027 built around the Accenture prescription. Executive accountability workshops. Board-level oversight rituals. AI governance committees with quarterly reporting cadences. All of it built on top of stacks where the substrate that would make the reporting mean anything does not exist.

The consulting layer will get paid. The gap will stay open. Some agent action will cascade into a nine-figure incident and the retrospective will say the leader should have owned it harder.

The retrospective will be wrong the same way the report is wrong. The leader could not have owned what the stack never recorded.

We are building the layer that records it. Not because coordination is a nice feature. Because accountability at agent speed is not a leadership trait. It is a property of the substrate — or it is nothing at all.

Intelligence may be scalable, but accountability is not.

That is not the ceiling of the argument. It is the entry point. The report named the gap. The fix belongs one layer down.
